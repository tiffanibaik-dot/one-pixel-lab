How does it learn?

Read the explanation (contains clues)

This classifier learned from six hearts labeled “love”—including a shallow-dip heart, a narrow heart, and an uneven heart—and a circle, oval, and square labeled “fake love.” For each new shape, it compares a tiny black-and-white picture with its examples and also checks for a dip between two higher sides at the top. The top-divot feature contributes 70% of the score; similarity to the examples contributes 30%. This is called nearest-neighbor classification.

It resizes shapes before comparing them, so a small heart and a big heart can both look like “love.” It uses the shape’s picture, not its name.

The love and fake love scores show how closely the picture matches each group of examples. They add up to 100%. An exact match to a learned heart with a top divot scores 100% love. Without a top divot, the love score cannot exceed 30%. More varied examples help it recognize unusual hearts, but unfamiliar shapes can still confuse it. These are similarity scores, not real probabilities.

Quiz points reward agreement with the classifier. It can make mistakes, so disagreeing doesn’t necessarily mean your guess is wrong. The lesson: its answers come from its examples. This is a shape game, not a way to judge real love, people, or feelings.
